MACG: Mobility-Aware Clustered Routing for UAV Networks

How can grouping mobile UAVs help data reach its destination? This team project studies Mobility-Aware Clustered Greedy Routing (MACG) and compares it with DSDV, Greedy, QGeo, and CR-QGeo using Python-based simulation.

Research Background: GMDC

Cai et al.'s GMDC is related work that informed the team's exploration of mobility-aware clustering. It groups UAVs using both distance and velocity alignment, then applies a mixed-strategy game to select cluster-head and gateway roles. The paper also describes TDMA scheduling and heartbeat-based maintenance. These are GMDC mechanisms; they should not be assumed to be implemented in MACG.

In its reported 200-node experiments, the paper reports average network-lifetime improvements of 25.1% over GDCR, 18.3% over K-means, and 38.0% over WCA. It also reports average residual energy of at least 40% after 600 rounds and lower role switching and maintenance frequency while keeping packet loss below 1%.

These are results reported for GMDC in the cited paper, not results from the team's MACG simulations. See the full citation.

How MACG Works

MACG organizes UAVs with similar positions and movement into clusters. Cluster heads and gateways support hierarchical forwarding, while ordinary Greedy rules select forwarding neighbors. Mobility information helps organize the clusters rather than replacing the distance-based Greedy rule.

01 · Form Groups

Use neighbor position and movement information to organize nearby UAVs into clusters.

02 · Assign Roles

Cluster heads organize groups, members join them, and gateways connect different clusters.

03 · Forward Packets

Use local, cluster-head, or gateway forwarding as appropriate, with flat Greedy fallbacks.

04 · Maintain Connections

Update cluster membership and gateway relationships as UAVs move and links change.

Implementation: MACG forwarding and cluster management. The repository describes the implementation as GMDC-inspired.

Results & Interpretation

Results Are Scenario-Dependent

In the team-reported 120-second UAV-count sweep using seed 2025, Greedy led PDR at 10, 20, and 50 UAVs, while MACG led at 100 UAVs. In the map-size sweep, MACG also had higher PDR than Greedy with 50 UAVs in a 300 m square area. These results describe the tested scenarios; they do not establish a general performance advantage.

Selected PDR comparisons from the team reports
ScenarioMACGGreedyReport
100 UAVs · 600 m square map10.37%3.11%UAV-count sweep
50 UAVs · 300 m square map38.66%25.04%Map-size sweep
10 UAVs · 1600 m square map8.80%19.68%Map-size sweep

The team reports interpret this pattern as a trade-off: dense networks provide more candidates for useful clusters, while sparse networks may not gain enough forwarding benefit to offset cluster setup and maintenance. The low absolute PDR at 100 UAVs also shows that the network remains heavily stressed.

Mobility Also Has a Maintenance Cost

In the 120-second speed sweep, Greedy led PDR at every tested speed. MACG's PDR was lowest at 20 m/s (23.30%) and higher at 30 and 40 m/s (38.84% and 40.99%). The report relates this non-monotonic pattern to clustering dynamics; it does not establish that MACG always improves with speed.

Read Delivery and Delay Together

Average delay describes delivered packets. A protocol can report a low delay while delivering very few packets, so delay alone does not indicate a better network.
